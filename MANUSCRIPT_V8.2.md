**One-sentence summary:** An open national model finds that electrification, biofuels and reactor-heat hydrogen could supply U.S. transport for less than petroleum, before and after natural gas runs short.

## Abstract

Replacing petroleum in U.S. transport requires electricity for most road travel and low-carbon liquids for aviation and shipping. We built an open, annual national cost model that links electric vehicles and roads, land biomass, optional offshore wind and seaweed, and high-temperature reactor hubs that supply heat and hydrogen. The least-cost 2050 system costs US\$455 billion per year (2026 dollars), against US\$593 billion for petroleum at late-2025 pre-tax prices, and cuts well-to-wheel emissions by 94%; it is cheaper in 96% of 1,000 Monte Carlo draws. When methane is no longer used, steam electrolysis raises the cost to US\$496 billion per year, and a hypothetical breeder fleet holds it near US\$499 billion. Reactor waste heat could also grow vegetables and seafood.

## Introduction

U.S. transport uses about 29 quadrillion Btu of energy per year, almost all of it petroleum, and we estimate its well-to-wheel emissions at about 2.9 Gt CO~2~e per year (*1*, *3*). Most light-duty travel can be electrified, but aviation, much of shipping and some off-road work will still need dense liquid fuels (*4*, *5*, *26*). Plans that treat one mode, fuel or generator at a time can therefore miss the binding limits, which sit in the shared supply of electricity, hydrogen and biomass and in the heat needed to process them (*5*, *27*).

We ask whether one national system can meet all 2050 U.S. transport services for less than petroleum, and what it would cost once natural gas is no longer available for hydrogen. The model fixes technology shares by segment, then chooses the least-cost mix of power, hydrogen routes, biomass, fuel-conversion routes and optional offshore wind with seaweed cultivation. High-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) hubs supply heat for methane pyrolysis, biocrude upgrading and drying, and their low-grade heat is sent to greenhouses and recirculating aquaculture.

Three questions follow from this design. First, whether the least-cost system is cheaper than petroleum across plausible parameter ranges. Second, how long U.S. and Canadian gas can support methane pyrolysis, and what replaces it. Third, whether a future closed-fuel-cycle reactor fleet could carry hydrogen, electricity and fuel upgrading indefinitely. We also report the food that reactor waste heat could grow, keeping its costs and benefits outside the transport comparison. All results are conditional screening estimates, not deployment forecasts.

## Results

### A least-cost system that is cheaper than petroleum

In the gas-era least-cost case, the transport system costs US\$455.2 billion per year (Fig. 1, Fig. 4A and Table 1). The same services at late-2025 pre-tax petroleum prices cost US\$592.8 billion, and US\$881.0 billion at November 2026 prices. The system needs 1,377 TWh of transport electricity, 118 TWh of transport hydrogen and 1,556 TWh of liquid fuels, of which about 1,000 TWh is jet fuel. Electricity comes from 155 GW of onshore wind (543 TWh), 263 GW of solar (575 TWh) and 203 four-module HTGR hubs (466 TWh). The hubs also produce 12.8 Mt of hydrogen per year by methane pyrolysis, using 2.71 trillion cubic feet (Tcf) of gas. Well-to-wheel emissions fall from 2,872.5 to 178.2 Mt CO~2~e per year (93.8%), and 141 Mt CO~2~-equivalent of solid carbon is kept out of the atmosphere as pyrolysis carbon, sold into capped markets or stored (fig. S7). A phased transition starting in 2026 would avoid about 33 Gt CO~2~e by 2050 (fig. S8).

The optimiser builds no offshore wind (Fig. 3). Imposing 220 GW of offshore wind with coupled seaweed farms, as a diversification case, raises cost to US\$506.1 billion per year. A 515 GW build-out raises it to US\$590.3 billion, about equal to petroleum. Offshore seaweed therefore has to be justified by benefits that the cost model does not price, such as supply diversity or coastal nutrient removal. The seaweed harvest in these cases assumes a proposed low-energy harvester that has not been field-tested (fig. S1).

### The cost advantage holds across 1,000 parameter draws

We drew 1,000 parameter sets per case over technology costs, reactor capital, biomass and seaweed yields, demand growth, fuel prices and carbon-market values (Fig. 4B and table S5). The least-cost system had a median cost of US\$504 billion (5th to 95th percentile, 377 to 641) and was cheaper than late-2025 petroleum in 96.2% of draws. The 220 GW case had a median of US\$574 billion (457 to 700) and was cheaper in 83.5% of draws; the 515 GW case, a median of US\$673 billion (558 to 794) and 33.3%. All three were cheaper than petroleum at November 2026 prices in every draw. Across the draws, the emission reduction stayed between 91% and 94%. In the 515 GW case, cost was most sensitive to the net annual cost of battery-electric light vehicles (Spearman ρ = 0.82), followed by service-demand growth (0.36) and fixed and floating offshore-wind cost (0.26 and 0.21).

### Gas supply for pyrolysis lasts into the late 2080s

Methane pyrolysis makes hydrogen without direct CO~2~ but consumes gas. We tracked U.S. and Canadian gas year by year using the Potential Gas Committee resource convention, 80% recovery and a 15-year reserves floor (*51*, *52*). With the pyrolysis programme, total U.S. demand first exceeds supply in 2086, two years earlier than without it (fig. S8). Converting building space and water heating to heat pumps (90% adoption by 2050) saves about 6.3 Tcf per year and pushes the shortfall to 2096 when their electricity is clean (*53*). Biogas from manure, wastewater, food waste and landfills adds at most one year (*54*). Without Canadian imports the shortfall arrives in 2082; with all expanded Canadian production plus heat pumps, in 2107. The date depends most on the recovery factor: 60% and 100% recovery give 2068 and 2104 (fig. S9 and table S6).

### After the gas: steam electrolysis costs 9% more

When methane is no longer available, the model switches to high-temperature steam electrolysis (HTSE) at the reactor hubs, at 36.8 kWh of electricity plus 6.4 kWh of heat per kilogram of hydrogen (*23*). Hydrogen production falls to 7.7 Mt per year because blending into building gas ends. The post-gas least-cost system costs US\$495.6 billion per year with 251 hubs (Table 1, fig. S11 and table S7), US\$33.3 billion more than the gas-era system on the same transport-only boundary without building-gas blending (US\$462.4 billion; fig. S10). Emissions fall to 131.5 Mt CO~2~e, a 95.4% reduction, because natural gas is no longer burned or leaked in hydrogen production and pipeline compression. Higher HTSE electricity use (42 to 48 kWh/kg) or 50% higher HTSE capital raises cost to US\$498.6 to 505.1 billion. Reactor capital is the largest uncertainty: low and high reactor costs give US\$445.5 and 513.5 billion, and at high cost the optimiser builds no hubs and makes hydrogen from wind and solar instead. The 220 GW and 515 GW post-gas cases cost US\$547.2 and 614.8 billion.

### A theoretical sustainable-fission fleet

Once-through fuel for a large reactor fleet would draw on finite uranium. We therefore modelled a speculative future in which 950°C very-high-temperature breeder reactors with full fuel recycling become commercial (*24*). This is a stress test, not a forecast. The breeder hubs make hydrogen by HTSE, generate electricity and supply steam for biocrude upgrading, and spare extraction steam returns to the turbines. Because the fleet cost curve with learning is not convex, we searched every whole number of hubs from 0 to 2,000.

The optimal fleet has 728 hubs and costs US\$498.9 billion per year; it supplies 1,844 TWh of electricity and nearly displaces wind and solar (fig. S12). Requiring nuclear-only supply gives the same cost (US\$498.9 billion, 729 hubs). Raising breeder capital cost by 25% and fuel-cycle cost from US\$12 to 15 per MWh of heat raises cost to US\$529.3 billion, still below late-2025 petroleum. Cheap sulfur–iodine thermochemical hydrogen, which needs the higher reactor outlet temperature, gives US\$498.5 billion. The fleet produces 4,751 TWh of heat per year, about 233 GW of electric-equivalent capacity, and needs about 213 t of heavy metal per year, 192 times less than the 40,930 t of natural uranium it would need once-through. At that rate, U.S. depleted uranium would last about 2,200 years and U.S. thorium about 2,800 years; world identified uranium would last only about 200 years without breeding (*20*, *55*). The binding limit is start-up fuel: about 2,333 t of fissile plutonium, 2.5 times the plutonium in U.S. spent fuel (fig. S13).

### Food from reactor heat

Low-grade heat from the gas-era hubs could support 8.0 Mt per year of greenhouse vegetables on about 17,800 ha and 1.0 Mt of farmed fish and shrimp, and could dry 60 Mt of grain (fig. S6). Grain drying preserves an existing harvest rather than adding food. After assumed edible fractions of 85% for vegetables and 60% for seafood, the produce equals the daily food energy of about 2.6 million people at 2,200 kcal per day, not complete diets. A further 2.5 Mt of hemp press meal could become food or feed after processing (*40*, *47*).

Nutrients recovered from the fish farms could partly feed tank-grown *Asparagopsis* seaweed, a cattle-feed additive that reduces enteric methane (*15*–*19*). An elemental balance of 84 kt nitrogen and 18 kt phosphorus in aquafeed recovers 27.5 kt N and 7.6 kt P after treatment and delivery losses (figs. S14 to S16). Under a 35% site-eligibility assumption, nitrogen limits production to 267 kt of dry seaweed per year, 62% of the 431 kt target, without added nutrients; the full target needs 5.9 kt N per year more. The nutrient-limited seaweed corresponds to a potential 27.9 Mt CO~2~e reduction in enteric methane, falling to 13 to 14 Mt under lower potency or participation (fig. S17). This response is a scenario multiplier, because the published bromoform dose–response analysis has been corrected (*16*, *17*). The food and nutrient systems would cost about US\$15.4 billion per year, including US\$21.9 million for nutrient treatment, against US\$21.4 billion in assumed produce sales. These amounts are kept outside the transport costs above.

## Discussion

Across the cases we tested, a U.S. transport system built on electric vehicles and roads, land biofuels and reactor-heat hydrogen is cheaper than petroleum at late-2025 prices in most parameter draws. The result does not depend on offshore wind: forcing 220 GW of offshore wind and seaweed adds about US\$51 billion per year. Most of the cost advantage comes from electrifying road transport. In the large offshore case, the net cost of battery-electric light vehicles drives more of the uncertainty than any reactor or fuel parameter.

The reactor hubs serve three roles that wind and solar cannot fill alone. They provide firm electricity, process heat for biocrude upgrading and drying, and hydrogen. In the gas era, pyrolysis hydrogen is cheap and keeps carbon solid. The gas analysis indicates that this is a bridge of roughly six decades, not a permanent supply. After it, HTSE keeps the system within about 9% of the gas-era cost and still below petroleum. If breeder reactors with full recycling become commercial, the same services could be supplied for about US\$499 billion per year for thousands of years from U.S. depleted uranium and thorium. Start-up fissile material, not long-run resources, limits how fast such a fleet could be built.

Several limits apply. The model balances energy annually and cannot show hourly adequacy, storage, ramping, winter peaks or transmission siting; firm-capacity and solar-share constraints are only surrogates (*31*, *32*). Liquid-fuel yields at national scale, jet-fuel certification and refinery build-out are assumed, not demonstrated (*28*–*30*, *48*). The gas-era configuration uses electric boosting above the 750°C reactor outlet, and the November 2026 construction permit for an Xe-100 project authorizes construction, not breeder operation (*46*). Very-high-temperature breeders, fuel recycling and their costs are speculative, and plutonium in spent fuel is not qualified reactor fuel. Upstream methane leakage may exceed inventory estimates (*45*). The petroleum comparison uses fixed historical prices, not a 2050 price forecast. Food, nutrient recovery and livestock outcomes need site-level engineering, contaminant control and feed approval before they could be claimed (*42*–*44*, *49*).

Within these limits, the model provides a transparent and reproducible accounting of what a petroleum-free U.S. transport system would require in electricity, heat, hydrogen, biomass and fuel. It identifies reactor capital, light-vehicle cost, gas recovery and start-up fissile supply as the parameters that most need better data.

## Materials and Methods

The model (us2_model.py, us2_inputs.py) is an annual linear programme solved with SciPy and HiGHS (*21*, *22*). It minimises annual cost in 2026 U.S. dollars, subject to balances for electricity, hydrogen, liquid fuels and jet fuel, and to bounds on biomass tiers, land, renewable capacity, offshore zones, carbon markets and heat sinks. Demand is built from EIA consumption data and fixed 2050 technology shares for nine segments (*1*, *25*; tables S1 and S2). Power and fuel-conversion parameters are given in tables S3 and S4. Biomass ceilings follow the 2023 Billion-Ton Report (*2*). Reactor fleet cost follows a Wright learning curve (*8*–*10*; fig. S5); the fleet size and unit cost are solved iteratively, and the sustainable-fission cases use an exhaustive search over 0 to 2,000 hubs. Pyrolysis, HTSE, sulfur–iodine and conventional electrolysis routes are temperature-checked against the reactor outlet (*6*, *7*, *23*). Emissions use well-to-wheel factors with 1.5% upstream methane leakage (*3*, *45*). Uncertainty uses 1,000 seeded triangular draws per case (seeds 11, 13 and 7 for the 0, 220 and 515 GW cases). The gas-depletion, fuel-supply, food, nutrient and community-cost modules are separate scripts. All parameters, equations and checks are given in the supplementary materials (S1 to S18), and the full run reproduces every output table byte for byte.
